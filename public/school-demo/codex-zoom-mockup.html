II

The directory should preserve the pleasure of browsing.

Caption: prototype notes for the Nanasays reading room

Independent-school search is usually compressed into a narrow funnel: a form, a ranking, a list of cards whose differences are flattened by identical templates. That is useful when a family already knows what it is looking for. It is less useful at the beginning, when the work is not yet comparison but orientation. A good directory should let a parent notice patterns before asking for filters. It should make geography, atmosphere, rhythm and institutional personality legible without demanding a spreadsheet mindset from the first click.

This wall treats the catalogue as a field of visual evidence. From a distance, the schools read as one archive: greens, brick, chapel stone, quadrangles, long drives, water, windows, playing fields. Zooming in changes the task. Names become available. Counties, boarding models and headline stats emerge only when the viewer has chosen a region of attention. That staged disclosure is deliberate. It keeps the first encounter generous and then rewards intent with detail, the way a museum label waits beside the object rather than shouting over the room.

The fly-in card is the bridge between atmosphere and decision. It does not ask the photograph to do analytical work alone; it turns a clicked tile into a concise school file with type, county, fee or outcome signal, setting and editorial note. For Nanasays, the pattern is important: the product can be rigorous without becoming cold. It can hold data, search and guidance inside a calmer visual system that respects the stakes of choosing a school while still making the act of discovery feel human.
